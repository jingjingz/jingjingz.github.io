Why is adjusting only Y insufficient?

Show explanation

The original predictor still contains variation shared with the other predictor. Residualizing both axes isolates the variation used to estimate the adjusted coefficient.

Explained variation cannot simply be added.

Correlated predictors can explain overlapping variation in Y. A second predictor explains a fraction of what the first leaves behind.

Does reversing the order change the final R²?

Show explanation

No. The final model contains the same predictors and has the same fitted values. The amount credited to each step changes because the first predictor gets credit for variation shared by both.

How can twelve-dimensional vectors fit in this picture?

Center y and each predictor to remove the intercept direction. The centered predictors span a two-dimensional plane. The full-model OLS residual is perpendicular to that plane. These three directions contain the centered response exactly for this example.

The plot uses orthonormal directions q₁, q₂ and q₃, so vector lengths and dot products are preserved in the three-dimensional geometry. Rotating the view changes their appearance on the screen, not their values.

Why does the mean-only projection become a point here?

Show explanation

In the original response space, the mean-only fit is the vector ȳ1 on the line span(1). After subtracting ȳ1, that fitted vector is zero. Its residual is the entire centered response, so SSreg = 0 and RSS = SYY.

What SE measures

SE estimates the sampling variability of the coefficient. Under E(e | X) = 0, OLS is unbiased; under Var(e | X) = σ²I, Cov(β̂ | X) = σ²(X′X)⁻¹.

When the t calculation applies

Exact t inference assumes a correct linear mean, independent normal errors with constant variance, and full column rank. Large-sample approximations require suitable regularity conditions.

Does a 95% interval give a 95% probability that this fixed coefficient is inside it?

Show explanation

No. Under the model assumptions, 95% of intervals constructed this way across repeated samples cover the fixed population coefficient.

Assumptions and interpretation

These are pointwise, model-based intervals. Exact coverage assumes a correct linear mean and independent normal errors with constant variance. A large sample can support approximate inference for the mean, but does not by itself make a normal-theory prediction interval valid for a new outcome.

A country-level life expectancy is the outcome here; this is not an interval for an individual woman’s lifespan. Intervals do not account for model misspecification.
